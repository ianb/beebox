# The dumping ground for cut material: names, practices, tools

Research notes on the drafting technique where cut material accumulates (typically at the bottom of the working document) instead of being deleted. 2026-07-20.

## 1. What it's called

There is no single canonical term. The names cluster by community, and the strength of attestation varies a lot.

### Genuinely attested terms of art

- **Boneyard** — the real screenwriting/production term, and the strongest "term of art" of the bunch. It comes from production usage (scenes shot or scheduled but not in the current cut/shooting script) and migrated into writing practice; it's common enough that Final Draft named a feature after it. See [What Is The Boneyard In Screenwriting? (Filmlifestyle)](https://filmlifestyle.com/what-is-the-boneyard-in-screenwriting/) and StudioBinder's production-side history, [Tales From The Boneyard: Knowing Where the Scenes Are Buried](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-the-boneyard-stripboard/), which ties it to the physical stripboard: strips for unscheduled/cut scenes were parked in a "boneyard" section of the board. Final Draft's own docs use the term casually as known vocabulary: "a repository for your script … a production term used for scenes not in the shooting script" ([Final Draft: Boneyard In the Beat Board](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/tech-tips-boneyard-in-the-beat-board)). Also used by book writers, e.g. [The writer's boneyard and other hacks to book writing (Pass the Chipotle)](https://www.passthechipotle.com/the-writers-boneyard-and-other-hacks-to-book-writing/).
- **Graveyard** — widespread among fiction writers, and now also a product feature name ([WriterDuet's paid "Graveyard" feature](https://www.writerduet.com/article/199-graveyard)). Practitioner account: [Lucy Flint, "Here's Why You Need a Writing Graveyard"](http://www.lucyflint.com/blog-1/graveyard-for-killed-darlings). Variants: "Darling Graveyard" ([Writers Helping Writers, "Writers, Grab a Knife"](https://writershelpingwriters.net/2022/01/writers-grab-a-knife-how-to-kill-your-darlings/)).
- **Outtake file / outtakes** — borrowed from film editing; used by novelists. Practitioner account: [Miriam Herin, "Tips for Writers: The Outtake File"](http://miriamherin.com/tips-for-writers-the-outtake-file/) — "a reservoir to which I return again and again."
- **Cut file / cut folder / deleted-scenes folder** — the plain descriptive names, dominant in the Scrivener world ("Cut," "Unused Scenes," "Deleted Scenes" folders in the binder). See [Literature & Latte, "Different Ways of Setting Up Scrivener's Binder"](https://www.literatureandlatte.com/blog/different-ways-of-setting-up-scriveners-binder-for-your-projects) and [Gwen Hernandez, "Snapshots and Unused Scenes in Scrivener"](https://gwenhernandez.com/2010/02/16/tech-tuesday-snapshots-and-unused-scenes-in-scrivener/).
- **Scrap file** — attested in revision-advice writing, with the nice framing "like kitchen scraps that can become a different meal later"; example file names "Cut Jewels," "Scrap" ([Mountford, "13 Tips to Ease Revision"](https://www.mountfordwriting.com/writing-advice/13-tips-to-ease-your-revision-woes)).
- **The Bin** — Highland 2's feature name (John August), consciously borrowed from non-linear video editing (Avid/Final Cut bins). See §4.

### One-off / idiosyncratic (real people, but personal coinages)

- **"Candy Bag"** — Mike Schur's writers-room term on *The Good Place* for cut subplots/characters/jokes; when the room was stuck he'd send writers to the candy bag. Reported (with several other writers' personal names: Nate Martins's "piggy bank," Rochi Zalani's "things cut" section) in the best single survey piece I found: [Reproof, "Don't kill your darlings: What writers do with the things they cut"](https://www.reproof.app/blog/writing-and-the-cutting-room-floor).
- **"Cutting room floor"** — common as a metaphor (as in the Reproof title) but not the name of the file itself.

### Candidate names that turned out to be something else

- **Morgue file** (journalism) — real term, wrong referent. A newspaper morgue is the *reference archive*: clippings, photos, background files on people and subjects, kept for future stories — not a writer's cut material ([Wikipedia: Morgue file](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morgue_file); [Smithsonian Archives, "Saving the Treasures from the Morgue"](https://siarchives.si.edu/blog/saving-treasures-morgue)). Adjacent in spirit (kept material as a future resource) but a different practice.
- **Spark file** (Steven Johnson) — real and well-documented, but it's an *ideas-capture* file (hunches, not cuts): one chronological Google Doc, no taxonomy, ~50 pages. Its killer feature is the discipline of **rereading the whole thing every 3–4 months** — which is exactly the missing piece in most cut-file practices (see §2). [Steven Johnson, "The Spark File"](https://medium.com/the-writers-room/the-spark-file-8d6e7df7ae58); follow-up: ["How Do You Capture Your Hunches?"](https://adjacentpossible.substack.com/p/how-do-you-capture-your-hunches).
- **Composting** (Natalie Goldberg) — real term from *Writing Down the Bones*, but it's a metaphor for how raw experience decomposes into usable material over time ("a bright red tulip shot out of the compost"), i.e. pre-writing digestion, not a file of cuts ([Goldberg, "Composting" excerpt (PDF)](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/534bed3ee4b09ed6294bec08/t/630637e7328962245fa8021d/1661351912398/composting-goldberg.pdf)). People do occasionally name their scrap file "compost heap," but I found no attestation of that as a shared term.
- **Slush** — means the unsolicited-submissions pile at publishers; not this.
- **Parking lot** — meeting-facilitation jargon (deferred agenda items); folk usage when applied to drafting, not attested as a writing term.
- **"Bone pile," "the basement," "holding pen," "darlings file"** — I found no evidence these are shared terms; treat as plausible-sounding inventions.

## 2. How people actually organize it

The Reproof survey ([Don't kill your darlings](https://www.reproof.app/blog/writing-and-the-cutting-room-floor)) is the best cross-section; it identifies three basic strategies — move cuts to the end of the document, keep a separate cuts document, or delete outright — with named practitioners for each:

- **Bottom-of-document, temporary** — Deb Tennen (Zapier editor) dumps rejected material at the bottom *while drafting*, then deletes the pile when the piece is done. Adam DuVander does the same: bottom of doc, then reintegrate or purge at finalization. For these writers the dump is scaffolding, not an archive — it exists to lower the cost of cutting during the draft, and the retrieval problem is solved by never keeping it long.
- **Separate per-project file, permanent** — Lucy Flint creates a "twin file" *at the moment she creates any new document*, holding "every deleted chunk of that project," down to phrases. Retrieval is text search: "If I change my mind later, I can search for it and pop it back into place." ([Lucy Flint](http://www.lucyflint.com/blog-1/graveyard-for-killed-darlings))
- **One career-spanning reservoir** — Miriam Herin keeps a single outtake file across manuscripts and reports actual cross-project reuse: "occasionally I've discovered that a piece I've cut from one manuscript fits perfectly into a different manuscript." She also keeps every draft and all research forever. ([Miriam Herin](http://miriamherin.com/tips-for-writers-the-outtake-file/))
- **Structured folders inside the tool** — Scrivener users keep a "Cut" / "Unused Scenes" folder as a sibling of the manuscript, one document per cut scene (so cuts stay chunked and titled, not a run-on pile), often paired with Snapshots for within-document history ([Gwen Hernandez](https://gwenhernandez.com/2010/02/16/tech-tuesday-snapshots-and-unused-scenes-in-scrivener/); [Literature & Latte binder setups](https://www.literatureandlatte.com/blog/different-ways-of-setting-up-scriveners-binder-for-your-projects)). A repeatedly-cited detail: material outside the Draft folder is excluded from compile and from word-count targets — the cut pile must not pollute progress statistics.
- **Renamed and color-coded beats** — Final Draft's Boneyard workflow: each cut becomes a named, color-codable card on the Beat Board, viewable in a split pane beside the script ([Final Draft](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/tech-tips-boneyard-in-the-beat-board)).

**The retrieval problem.** Nobody I found tags cuts by *why* they were cut; dating is implicit (paste order). The honest answers to "does material come back?" split three ways: (a) search, when you remember a phrase (Flint); (b) periodic rereading as deliberate practice — attested for idea files (Johnson's every-3-4-months reread, where he finds "the idea I had in 2008 that made no sense in 2008" now usable) but rarely claimed for cut files; (c) admission that it mostly doesn't come back, and the file's real function is psychological — Reproof quotes writers conceding the outtakes doc gives "the ego the illusion they haven't deleted words — just moved them." Deb Tennen's delete-at-the-end variant is the intellectually honest version of that position.

## 3. Why it works

- **Lowering the cost of cutting.** This is the near-universal claim, in almost identical words across sources. Flint: without the graveyard she felt "wild-eyed panic of tossing my beautiful little words out to the wolves"; with it she's "much quicker to chop things that really, really needed to go." John August on the Bin: it "lets writers be more aggressive about cutting stuff because they know they can always put it back in" ([LA Screenwriter interview](https://la-screenwriter.com/2019/06/24/john-august-on-highland-2-5-and-writing-the-movie-you-want-to-see/)). Filmlifestyle on the boneyard: "empowers writers to cut liberally … secure in the knowledge that no idea is ever truly lost." The mechanism is loss-aversion management: convert an irreversible loss into a reversible move, and the flinch disappears.
- **Kill your darlings — the actual source.** Arthur Quiller-Couch, Cambridge lectures 1913–14, *On the Art of Writing*: "Whenever you feel an impulse to perpetrate a piece of exceptionally fine writing, obey it—whole-heartedly—and delete it before sending your manuscript to press. Murder your darlings." Routinely misattributed to Faulkner, Wilde, Chekhov; Stephen King's *On Writing* popularized the "kill your darlings" phrasing. See [Slate's history of the phrase](https://slate.com/culture/2013/10/kill-your-darlings-writing-advice-what-writer-really-said-to-murder-your-babies.html) and [Lisa Spangenberg's sourcing](https://www.lisaspangenberg.com/writing/murder-your-darlings/). The cut-file practice is explicitly framed by many writers as the *enabler* of Quiller-Couch's advice — you can murder them because they're only sleeping. A notable dissent: John Crowley's ["Spare the Darling" (Harper's)](https://harpers.org/archive/2014/11/spare-the-darling/) argues the maxim is over-applied.
- **Revision as recursive, not linear.** The classic composition-studies result: Nancy Sommers, ["Revision Strategies of Student Writers and Experienced Adult Writers" (CCC, 1980)](https://publicationsncte.org/content/journals/10.58680/ccc198015930) — experienced writers revise recursively via deletion/substitution/addition/reordering and treat revision as re-seeing the whole, where students only reword. A cut pile is infrastructure for exactly that recursive movement (material leaves and re-enters across passes). I found **no** empirical composition-studies work specifically on saved-cut-text reuse; it appears genuinely understudied.

## 4. Tools with an explicit cut-but-keep affordance

- **Highland 2 — "the Bin"** (John August). The most deliberate design. August, after watching film editors on Avid/Final Cut keep material in bins beside the timeline, built the Bin and Scratch Pad into Highland ([Humanitas, "Reinventing the Typewriter"](https://www.humanitasprize.org/blog/reinventingthetypewriter)). Interaction: drag text out of the document into a sidebar; drag it back to restore; export the Bin as its own file. August on writing his novel *Arlo Finch* this way: "there are bits of text you need to cut, but you also need to hold onto… I can just drag them back in" ([John August, "Writing Arlo Finch in Highland 2"](https://johnaugust.com/2018/how-and-why-to-write-a-novel-in-highland-2)). Key design point: adjacency — the cut material lives in a pane *next to* the text, not at the bottom or in another file.
- **Final Draft — Boneyard in the Beat Board.** Drag cut script text to the Beat Board; it becomes a formatted, renamable, color-codable Beat; split-screen view puts script and boneyard side by side; drag back to reintegrate with formatting intact ([Final Draft tech tip](https://www.finaldraft.com/blog/tech-tips-boneyard-in-the-beat-board)).
- **WriterDuet — Graveyard.** Dedicated cut-archive panel with first-class keyboard shortcuts: "Cut to Graveyard" (Shift+Cmd+X) and "Copy to Graveyard" alongside ordinary cut/copy — cut-but-keep as a peer of the clipboard ([WriterDuet knowledgebase](https://www.writerduet.com/article/199-graveyard)).
- **Scrivener.** No single "boneyard" feature; instead three composable mechanisms: (1) binder folders outside the Draft (cuts stay as titled documents, excluded from compile and targets); (2) **Snapshots** — per-document version checkpoints taken before risky edits, with rollback ([Gwen Hernandez](https://gwenhernandez.com/2010/02/16/tech-tuesday-snapshots-and-unused-scenes-in-scrivener/)); (3) per-document **include-in-compile** — a scene can sit *in place in the manuscript order* but be switched out of the output, which is the closest existing thing to "inline but excluded." Keith Blount's design premise is relevant background: Scrivener exists because "many, if not most, writers don't write long works from beginning to end" — the whole app is a ring-binder-and-index-cards model where membership in the final text is a property you toggle, not a location ([Binpress interview with Keith Blount](https://www.binpress.com/podcast-20-keith-blount-scrivener/); [Literature & Latte, "Integrating Scrivener's Binder, Corkboard, and Outliner"](https://www.literatureandlatte.com/blog/integrating-scriveners-binder-corkboard-and-outliner)).
- **Ulysses — Material Sheets.** A sheet can be marked as "material": it stays exactly where it is in the group/order, but is excluded from export, statistics, and writing goals ([Ulysses help: Sheets & Groups](https://help.ulysses.app/567894-sheets-groups)). This is the purest shipping example of the boxholder's "inline but not in the output" idea, at sheet granularity.
- **Org-mode (Emacs).** The `COMMENT` keyword on a heading excludes the whole subtree from export while it remains inline and foldable — cut-but-keep at any point in the outline, toggled with one command ([Org manual: Comment Lines](https://orgmode.org/manual/Comment-Lines.html)). Prose-writers-in-Emacs write-ups treat folding + narrowing as the standard way to keep dead text nearby but invisible ([Lucid Manager, "Emacs Org Mode for Distraction-Free Writing"](https://lucidmanager.org/productivity/emacs-for-distraction-free-writing/)).
- **Obsidian/PKM.** Nothing built in; users make a `Scraps/` folder by convention. The PKM world's real contribution is the *idea-file* side (spark-file-style capture with periodic review), not cut management.
- **Google Docs.** No affordance at all; hence the folk bottom-of-doc practice. (Version history preserves cuts in principle but is write-only in practice — the canonical "graveyard nobody reads.")

## 5. Non-linear and inline variants

- **Inline-but-excluded** (the boxholder's collapsed-inline idea) exists in shipping tools: Ulysses Material Sheets, Scrivener's include-in-compile checkbox, org-mode `COMMENT` subtrees, and — degenerately — commenting out prose in any markup format. Tradeoff as practitioners describe it: inline placement preserves *context* (you see where the cut came from and it can shame you back into using or deleting it), but clutters navigation and pollutes the reading flow of the draft; exile (bin/graveyard file) keeps the draft clean but severs the cut from its context, worsening retrieval. The side-pane designs (Highland Bin, Final Draft split-screen Boneyard, WriterDuet Graveyard widget) are explicitly a middle position: out of the text, but persistently visible next to it.
- **Card-based methods** handle rejection *positionally*: a card doesn't get deleted, it gets moved off the active structure. The stripboard boneyard is the physical original (strips parked in a reserved board section — [StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-the-boneyard-stripboard/)); Final Draft's Beat Board digitizes it; Scrivener's corkboard equivalent is dragging the index card to a non-Draft folder. John McPhee's structure practice is the deep practitioner account of card-shuffling composition — coded index cards on a wall, notes physically cut into slivers and sorted into folders, later replicated in the text editor Kedit with custom macros ([McPhee, "Structure" / *Draft No. 4* material; jsomers scan](https://jsomers.net/mcphee-draft-no-4.pdf); [Migurski's notes on McPhee's structure](http://mike.teczno.com/notes/john-mcphee-structure.html)) — though McPhee's system is about *arranging* material rather than warehousing rejects; in a sort-everything system, "rejected" is just "never got assigned a place."
- **Snowflake method**: nothing distinctive found on rejected-material handling; it's a top-down expansion method, and cuts revert to the design documents.

## Design notes (for a software affordance)

- The winning interaction in shipping tools is **drag out / drag back with a persistent adjacent pane**, not append-to-bottom: Highland's Bin, Final Draft's split-screen Boneyard, WriterDuet's Graveyard widget. Adjacency is what keeps the pile alive instead of becoming a write-only grave.
- Make cut-to-keep a **peer of the clipboard** (WriterDuet's Shift+Cmd+X) — one gesture, no file management.
- Cuts should stay **chunked and nameable** (Final Draft auto-creates a named, color-codable card per cut; Scrivener keeps one document per cut scene), not concatenated into an undifferentiated pile.
- **Exclude cut material from counts/exports automatically** (Scrivener compile, Ulysses material sheets) — if the pile inflates word counts or leaks into output, people delete it.
- The two honest failure modes to design against: the pile that's really just deferred deletion (support an explicit end-of-project sweep, à la Deb Tennen), and the grave nobody revisits (the only attested fix is *scheduled rereading*, Johnson-style — a prompt to re-read the pile at revision milestones would be a genuine advance over every tool surveyed).
- **Inline-but-excluded** (Ulysses material, org COMMENT, Scrivener include-in-compile) is the strongest existing model for the boxholder's collapsed-inline instinct: membership in the final text as a toggleable property of a chunk, rather than a location.

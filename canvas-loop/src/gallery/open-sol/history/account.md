# The Library That Crossed the Flood

I invented a book about a turtle who carries a tiny library from house to house after a valley floods. This page catches the crossing at dusk: the keeper holds up a lantern, warm books glow in the shell, and the dark houses wait on the far bank. I chose a slow, reassuring journey because the turtle's broad silhouette, an illuminated room, and reflective water offered a strong composition I could actually paint with these brushes. The open upper sky leaves breathing room for picture-book text.

The drawing fought me most at two scales. At page scale, my first row of trees became a mechanical picket fence and hid the houses. At close scale, the keeper was nearly a yellow oval with a red dot for a head. Fewer, varied trees and a more deliberate raincoat helped. The broad shell remains somewhat like a painted ring around a room, and the keeper still has simple folk-art anatomy. The library's BOOKS sign only reads in a zoom. These are limits of my drawing, not a renderer bug.

I used `curve` and `curvePath` for the animal, houses, hills and clothing, and `clip` to keep strokes inside the shell. Bristle marks carried its dry paint and the broken water reflections; wash softened clouds, moonlight and the turtle's highlights; ink supplied the silhouette and small character lines. `ellipseGradient` gave the lantern and room their glows, and `paperTexture` tied flat base colours to the marks. Pencil disappointed me on the reddish shell: the scute lines were almost invisible in cycle 9, so I replaced them with restrained ink in cycle 10. The transcript also warned that some distant water and bank marks were below useful opacity. I wanted easier layered, irregular wet-paint colour within a shape and more time to hand-place the many little scutes and ripples. Typecheck's strict indexed-array rules required explicit tuple types and fallbacks after the final render; those changes did not change the drawing.

## Ways of seeing

The whole frame was best for composition and story. It exposed the first tree fence and showed that the open bank in cycle 7 let the turtle separate from the village. Zooms were best for brush texture and character legibility: the keeper crop in cycle 4 showed coat seams and a face that were barely perceptible at page size, while the water crop in cycle 6 showed the first reflection was dull grey-brown. The diff highlight was best for locating small edits and checking that a revision stayed where intended; in cycle 8 it separated the brighter water marks from the much subtler light spilling on the shell. Diff change crops were best for inspecting tiny before and after details, especially the keeper's coat in cycle 4 and the library roof in cycle 5. Side by side was best for large balance changes, particularly the two versions of the wooded bank in cycle 7. I stopped relying on highlight for overall composition because it greys most of the picture, and I stopped trusting a zoom alone to tell me whether a detail mattered on the page.

I enjoyed the deterministic loop: each render let me judge a specific change without the rest of the picture wandering. Ten cycles gave the scene a readable mood and a warm path through dark water. I am pleased with the turtle's calm face and the lit books. It still lacks the loose, surprising asymmetry and confident anatomy of a strong hand-painted picture book; the water strokes and some shell seams remain visibly programmed. I would next redraw the keeper and vary the shell structure, rather than add more decoration.
